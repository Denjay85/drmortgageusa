HOA vs Non-HOA Homes in Orlando: What to Check

By Dennis Ross, NMLS #2018381|October 21, 2026

Two Orlando homes make your shortlist. One has an HOA. The other does not. Before deciding the non-HOA home is the cheaper choice, I would put the full budget and the responsibilities for each property next to each other.

My recommendation is simple: compare what you would pay, what you would maintain, and what you need permission to do. Do not let an attractive listing payment or a neighborhood label make that decision for you.

Separate the Mortgage Bill From the Housing Budget

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau says HOA dues are usually paid directly to the association, separate from the payment to your mortgage servicer. That means the amount leaving your account for the mortgage may not include the association bill.[1]

There is also a difference between how you pay a bill and how the lender evaluates it. For loans following Fannie Mae rules, monthly housing expense includes applicable owners' association dues along with principal, interest, taxes, insurance, and other specified housing obligations. For a principal residence, this housing expense is used in the debt-to-income calculation.[2] Ask your loan officer to confirm the treatment for your actual program.

I would request a property-specific estimate for each address. For related planning, read how much house you can afford in Orlando. A preapproval is not a substitute for checking the costs of the home you actually choose.

Ask What the Dues Cover, Not Just How Much They Are

For the HOA property, I would ask the seller or association for the current dues amount, billing schedule, approved budget, and a written explanation of the services included. Ask whether there are additional associations, approved increases, or special assessments to review. These are due-diligence questions, not a claim that every community has those charges.

Then check the details that matter to your household. Is lawn care included? Who handles the roof or exterior? Is a pool or other amenity included, optional, or billed separately? Ask for the documents supporting each answer rather than relying on a verbal description during a showing.

I would not treat a list of amenities as proof of value. If you would rarely use them, include that in your decision. If you want someone else handling certain maintenance, confirm exactly which responsibilities would remain yours.

Give the Non-HOA Home Its Own Maintenance Plan

For the non-HOA option, I would build a separate maintenance worksheet. Ask the inspector about the roof, exterior, drainage, major equipment, and other property-specific concerns. Obtain estimates where needed instead of assuming the absence of dues means there is nothing to budget.

Write down what you intend to handle yourself and what you would hire out. Add a realistic allowance based on the home's condition and your own estimates. There is no universal maintenance figure I would prescribe without knowing the property.

My preference is to compare homes after those questions are answered, not to declare either ownership setup automatically cheaper.

Review the Documents Before Relying on a Lifestyle Plan

Thinking about a fence, work vehicle, rental use, or an exterior change? I would give your agent a written list of your plans and ask which recorded documents, association rules, or local requirements need review. Do this for both homes.

Do not treat this article, an HOA label, or a non-HOA listing description as confirmation that your intended use is permitted. Ask a Florida real estate attorney to interpret restrictions, contract rights, or legal obligations when needed. This is a checklist for the conversation, not legal advice or a statement of Florida disclosure law.

Use the Same Comparison Sheet for Both Addresses

Here is the worksheet I would take to a conversation with your agent and loan officer:

  • Mortgage review: property-specific principal and interest estimate, taxes, insurance, and any applicable mortgage insurance or other financing payment.
  • Association questions: documented dues, payment frequency, included services, and any additional charges that need investigation.
  • Ownership plan: inspection findings, maintenance estimates, utilities, and services you would pay for yourself.
  • Cash plan: estimated closing costs, any verified association-related charges, and the cash you want left after closing.
  • Unresolved items: who will verify each answer and when you need it before making a commitment.

Keep lender qualifying expenses separate from your personal maintenance allowance. Ask what is included in each estimate so you do not count the same cost twice or leave it out entirely. For another useful planning check, read why your preapproval amount is not your real budget.

Choose With the Actual Costs in Front of You

I would not rule out an HOA home just because it has dues, and I would not stretch for a non-HOA home just because its listing looks simpler. Get the documents, price the responsibilities, and ask the lender to review each address. Then decide which setup fits the way you want to own a home.
