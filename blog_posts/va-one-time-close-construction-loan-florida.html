VA One-Time Close Construction Loans in Florida: Build With One Closing

By Dennis Ross, NMLS #2018381 | September 15, 2026

A lot of veterans know they can use a VA loan to buy a home. Far fewer know that the benefit may also help them build one.

A VA one-time close construction loan combines the construction financing and the permanent VA mortgage into one coordinated closing. Instead of taking a short-term construction loan and hoping to qualify again for a refinance after the home is finished, the permanent financing is built into the structure from the beginning.

That does not make construction simple. It makes the process more organized when the borrower, builder, land, plans, budget, appraisal, insurance, title work, and lender all fit the program.

Yes, a VA Loan Can Be Used to Build a Home

VA's current purchase-loan page says a VA-backed purchase loan can help an eligible borrower buy, build, or improve a home. The veteran still needs a valid Certificate of Eligibility, must satisfy VA and lender standards, and must intend to occupy the completed home as a primary residence.[2]

The hard part is usually not whether VA recognizes new construction. The hard part is finding a lender with a workable construction process and getting every moving piece approved before the project starts.

What “One-Time Close” Actually Means

With a one-time close structure, the veteran closes the construction loan and permanent mortgage together. Construction funds are released to the builder through an approved draw schedule. After the home is complete and the required final items are received, the construction loan is modified into the permanent mortgage.

Compare that with a two-time close:

  • The borrower or builder first obtains separate construction financing.
  • The borrower later applies for a new permanent mortgage.
  • A second approval, closing, and set of closing costs may be required.
  • Changes in income, credit, debt, appraisal, rates, or program rules can affect the second transaction.

One closing removes the need to refinance a separate construction loan after completion. It does not remove underwriting, project approval, inspections, documentation, or construction risk.

Buying the Land and Building at the Same Time

If the veteran is buying the lot at the same closing, the transaction is generally structured as a purchase. The land contract and construction contract may be combined or separate, but the lender needs a clear breakdown of the land cost and construction cost.

The veteran may buy the land from the builder or from a different seller. Either way, title, the legal description, the construction contract, and the final appraisal must all describe the same property.

Already Own the Land? The File Is Treated Differently

When the veteran already owns the lot before the one-time close loan closes, UWM's current guide treats the transaction as a refinance for system and underwriting purposes. Existing lot liens generally must be documented and paid through the transaction.

That refinance label does not mean the veteran can freely pull cash out of the construction loan. UWM's guide says the veteran generally cannot receive cash from loan proceeds, other than limited refunds of documented deposits that were paid outside the transaction and included correctly in the loan structure.[4]

Land equity can matter, including how the veteran's ownership period affects the funding-fee calculation when the veteran is not exempt. This is not a place to guess. The original land purchase, current liens, title, appraisal, and complete acquisition cost need to be reconciled.

The Builder and the Project Both Need Approval

The VA construction path is not just an approval of the veteran. The builder and the construction project must also work.

VA's Construction and Valuation page says a builder needs a VA Builder ID to sell properties with VA financing.[3] The lending partner may require a separate contractor package and project review even when the builder already has a VA ID.

The construction review can include:

  • Builder or contractor information and required certifications.
  • A detailed construction contract, plans, specifications, and completion date.
  • The project budget, draw schedule, and contingency planning.
  • Permits and the legal description or assigned property address.
  • Builder's risk insurance or acceptable dwelling-under-construction coverage.
  • Title requirements for future construction disbursements.
  • Progress inspections and final completion documentation.

A great veteran file cannot rescue a builder package that does not meet the program.

Read the Construction Contract Before You Sign It

The construction contract is not just a price sheet. UWM's current guide requires the contract to obligate the builder to finish according to the plans and specifications, include a completion date, identify the property accurately, and state who is responsible for special assessments or improvement bonds.

The current guide also flags several terms that can make the contract unacceptable unless corrected:

  • A builder deposit that remains nonrefundable if the veteran cannot qualify for VA financing.
  • A cost-escalation clause that raises the price without allowing the veteran to cancel and recover the deposit.
  • A clause prohibiting the veteran from selling the completed property.
  • A clause forcing the veteran to waive claims if the builder fails to complete the home.

Every change order must also be provided. If the construction cost changes, the appraisal, project approval, loan amount, or available funds may need to be updated.

The Home Cannot Be Too Far Along

This is designed for proposed or early-stage construction, not a renovation loan and not a backdoor refinance of a nearly completed home.

UWM's current guide says an existing property cannot be renovated with this VA one-time close product. It also says the foundation cannot already be completed for the file to be treated as a standard one-time close transaction. A completed foundation requires escalation and may not be accepted.

If work has already started, stop before assuming the project still fits. Photos, permits, builder invoices, inspections, and appraisal observations can expose a timing problem late in the file.

Property Types and Structures Need an Early Review

UWM's current guidance allows certain single-family, modular, manufactured, and unique-property scenarios, but each has additional requirements. Attached condominiums and cooperative properties are not eligible for this specific product. A barndominium or another unusual design may require strong comparable sales showing market acceptance.

Barns, sheds, garages, workshops, and other nonresidential structures are not automatically fatal, but the complete property must remain primarily residential and appraise acceptably.

The Appraisal Is Based on a Home That Does Not Exist Yet

The VA appraisal is ordered as proposed construction. The appraiser relies on the lot, plans, specifications, foundation or basement plan, exterior elevations, wall sections, and other construction exhibits to estimate the finished value.

The appraiser must certify that the listed construction exhibits were used. If the plans change, the contract price changes, or the proposed home no longer matches what was appraised, the appraisal may need to be updated.

Unique properties need acceptable comparable sales. A design that can be built is not automatically a design the appraiser can support.

No Monthly Mortgage Payment During Construction Does Not Mean No Cost

UWM's public program page says the veteran does not make monthly mortgage payments during the build period.[1] The current partner guide explains the other side of that benefit: construction-period interest, required property insurance, inspection fees, draw fees, and certain taxes are handled through the approved project budget and lender process.

Those costs are not magically erased. They must be calculated, documented, and budgeted correctly. If the interest rate, loan amount, draw schedule, insurance, or construction timeline changes, the construction calculations and project approval may need to be updated.

The veteran's permanent mortgage payments begin after the home is completed and the loan moves into its permanent phase.

Insurance, Title, and Draws Are Part of the Loan

The project needs coverage during construction. Depending on the structure, the file may use a builder's risk policy paired with a homeowner's insurance quote or a homeowner's policy with a dwelling-under-construction endorsement.

Title work also needs to support future advances as the lender releases construction funds. The closing is only the beginning of the disbursement process. Funds go out in draws after approved work and inspections, not as one unrestricted payment to the builder.

The lender's construction team controls the draw process. Borrower and builder expectations should match that process before closing.

What Happens After the Home Is Finished

Completion is not just the builder saying the home is done. The lender may need a certificate of occupancy or local equivalent, final draw documentation, inspections, warranties, builder certifications, pest-treatment forms when applicable, and confirmation that the completed property matches the approved plans and appraisal.

UWM's current guide requires post-construction documentation that can include a VA warranty of completion or an accepted long-term protection plan, builder certifications, and a veteran acknowledgement when VA did not inspect the property during construction.[4]

After the final requirements are satisfied, the construction loan is modified into the permanent mortgage and recorded through the lender's process.

Documents I Want Before Calling the Project “Ready”

  • The veteran's Certificate of Eligibility and basic qualification documents.
  • The county and exact land status: buying at closing, owned free and clear, or owned with a lien.
  • The builder's legal business name, contact information, and VA Builder ID status.
  • The complete construction contract, including every addendum and change order.
  • Plans, specifications, floor plans, elevations, and the proposed property description.
  • A realistic construction timeline and itemized project budget.
  • Documentation for deposits, prepaid materials, and land acquisition.
  • Insurance and title contacts who understand construction transactions.

UWM's current guide gives new-construction income, credit, and asset documents a longer validity window than a standard existing-home file, but long build timelines still create opportunities for documents and facts to change. The file needs to be managed from closing through completion.

Who This Can Fit

  • A veteran who wants a custom or semi-custom primary home instead of buying an existing property.
  • A veteran buying land and building in one coordinated transaction.
  • A veteran who already owns land and wants to use an eligible construction structure.
  • A veteran with a qualified builder, complete plans, and a realistic budget.
  • A borrower who wants to avoid qualifying for a separate permanent refinance after construction.

Who Should Slow Down Before Signing

  • The builder has not been screened for VA and lender requirements.
  • The contract contains a hard nonrefundable deposit or an uncontrolled escalation clause.
  • The foundation or home is already substantially complete.
  • The project is really a renovation of an existing home.
  • The budget leaves out insurance, interest, inspections, draws, permits, site work, or a contingency.
  • The veteran expects unrestricted cash back from land equity.
  • The property type or design has weak comparable sales.

The Bottom Line

Veterans are not limited to buying someone else's finished home. A VA one-time close construction loan may provide a path to buy land, build a primary residence, and transition into permanent financing through one coordinated mortgage closing.

The opportunity is real. So are the details. Builder approval, land ownership, contracts, plans, appraisal, insurance, title, draws, and final construction documents all matter.

Do not start with “Can I get a VA construction loan?” Start with four questions: Do I own the land? Who is the builder? Are the plans and budget complete? How far has construction already gone?
