Mortgage Underwriting Letters of Explanation for Florida Homebuyers

By Dennis Ross, NMLS #2018381|September 21, 2026

An underwriter asks for a letter of explanation because a fact in your mortgage file needs context. The letter should make that fact easier to verify. It should not turn into your life story.

For Florida and Orlando homebuyers, the best letter is short, specific, honest, and tied to documents. It answers the exact question, gives dates and amounts when relevant, and points to the proof attached with it.

The Short Version

  • Write to the exact underwriting condition, not the general situation.
  • State what happened, when it happened, and whether it is resolved.
  • Attach documents that support the explanation.
  • Do not guess, hide facts, blame people, or promise facts you cannot prove.
  • A letter explains evidence. It does not automatically replace required evidence.

What Is a Mortgage Letter of Explanation?

A mortgage letter of explanation, often called an LOE, is a written statement from the borrower that addresses a specific question in the loan file. The request may come from the loan officer, processor, or underwriter.

The letter is not a character reference and it is not a sales pitch. Its job is to connect a fact to the supporting paper trail so the lender can evaluate the complete file under the selected program and its own requirements.

Fannie Mae's current conventional guidance shows why context and documentation work together. Its depository-account rules allow a borrower's written explanation as one possible part of documentation for certain large deposits, but the lender still must document its rationale and verify sufficient acceptable funds.[1] In a different context, Fannie Mae requires a written explanation and supporting records when a borrower asks the lender to consider extenuating circumstances after certain derogatory credit events.[3]

The details change by program, lender, automated underwriting findings, and condition. That is why a borrowed template should never override the exact request from your loan team.

Common Reasons an Underwriter Requests a Letter

A letter may be requested when documents are accurate but do not tell the whole story. Common examples include:

  • Large or unusual bank deposits: A deposit may need an identified source, an explanation, and supporting records if the funds are relevant to the purchase.
  • Recent credit inquiries: The lender may need to determine whether a new account or debt resulted from the inquiry. Fannie Mae requires lenders to investigate recent inquiries shown on the credit report and confirm resulting debts.[2]
  • Address differences: The application, credit report, driver's license, tax documents, or bank statements may show different current or prior addresses.
  • Employment gaps or changes: A gap, job transition, change in pay structure, or move from employee to self-employed work may need context and separate income documentation.
  • Late payments or derogatory credit: The underwriter may ask what happened, when it happened, and whether the issue was isolated or ongoing.
  • Recurring transfers or payments: A transfer between your own accounts, a private obligation, or an automatic payment may need to be identified.
  • Name variations: A maiden name, shortened name, suffix, or reporting error may need a simple explanation and identity support.
  • Occupancy or property questions: The lender may need clarification about your intended use of the property, another address, or ownership of another home.

A request is not automatically bad news. It means the file contains a question that still needs an acceptable answer.

What to Put in the Letter

Start with the underwriting condition. If the request says, "Explain the $8,400 deposit dated August 12," the letter should identify that deposit directly. Do not send a vague note about saving money.

A clean letter usually includes:

  1. Your full name and the property address or loan number if the lender requests it.
  2. The date of the letter.
  3. A direct subject line naming the condition.
  4. A factual explanation with relevant dates, amounts, people, accounts, or events.
  5. A statement identifying the attached supporting documents.
  6. Your signature when the lender requires one.

Keep it tight. One focused paragraph is often better than a page of background that creates three new questions.

A Practical Letter Structure

Date: September 21, 2026

Subject: Explanation of August 12 deposit

To whom it may concern:

The $8,400 deposit into my checking account ending in 1234 on August 12, 2026, came from the sale of my 2019 vehicle. I sold the vehicle to [buyer name] on August 11, 2026. Attached are the signed bill of sale, a copy of the deposited cashier's check, and the account statement showing the deposit.

Sincerely,
[Borrower name]

This is an illustration, not a universal form. The actual source must be acceptable for the selected transaction, and the lender may ask for different or additional evidence. Never copy facts, dates, or document names that are not true for your file.

The Letter Is Only Half the Answer

A strong explanation points to evidence. Depending on the question, that evidence might include:

  • Complete bank statements showing the source and receipt of transferred funds
  • A bill of sale and proof of payment
  • Gift documentation requested by the lender
  • Closing documents from the sale of another property
  • An employer letter, offer letter, paystub, W-2, tax return, or verification of employment
  • A paid receipt, account statement, or creditor letter
  • A divorce decree, court order, insurance record, or other third-party document when relevant
  • Identity documents supporting a name or address explanation

Send complete, legible records. Do not crop away the account owner, dates, balances, page numbers, or transaction details. Do not edit a statement or cover information unless the loan team specifically approves the redaction.

What Not to Write

Bad letters usually fail in predictable ways:

  • Too vague: "The money came from savings" does not identify where the money was held or show the transfer.
  • Too much storytelling: Extra facts can create unrelated questions and slow review.
  • No documents: A confident explanation does not prove a deposit, debt payoff, job change, or past event.
  • Contradictions: Dates, amounts, names, and account numbers must match the records.
  • Blame or emotion: Underwriting needs facts it can evaluate, not an argument about fairness.
  • Invented certainty: Do not state that an issue is resolved unless the attached record proves it.
  • Reused internet language: A generic template can answer the wrong question or include facts that are not yours.

Do not alter a document to make the explanation cleaner. That can turn a manageable condition into a serious credibility problem.

Large Deposits Need More Than a Good Story

For a Fannie Mae purchase transaction, a large deposit that is needed for the down payment, closing costs, or reserves must be evaluated and documented as coming from an acceptable source. If the source remains undocumented, verified funds are reduced by that amount, and the lender must confirm that the remaining funds are sufficient.[1]

That does not mean every loan program treats every deposit the same way. It does mean buyers should preserve the source trail before moving money. If you transfer funds between accounts, keep both statements. If you sell an asset, keep the ownership record, sale agreement, and payment proof. If somebody gives you money, talk to the loan officer before accepting or depositing it.

Undocumented cash is especially hard to fix because a letter cannot create the missing source trail.

Recent Credit Inquiries Can Trigger Two Questions

First, why was your credit checked? Second, did the inquiry result in new credit or another obligation?

Answer both. If you shopped for insurance and no debt was created, say that plainly. If you opened an account, disclose it and provide the requested account terms or statement. Fannie Mae's conventional guidance says the lender must determine whether recent inquiries resulted in additional credit and qualify the borrower with the new debt when applicable.[2]

Leaving out the second question is a common mistake. The inquiry itself is not always the main issue. The possible new debt is.

How to Respond Without Creating More Conditions

  1. Ask for the exact wording. Work from the actual condition.
  2. Collect the evidence first. Your letter should match what the documents prove.
  3. Use exact dates and amounts. Verify them against the records before writing.
  4. Answer every part. A credit inquiry request may ask about both purpose and new debt.
  5. Keep separate issues separate. Use one labeled section or letter per condition when that makes review clearer.
  6. Review for conflicts. Compare the letter with your application, credit report, statements, and prior explanations.
  7. Confirm acceptance. Sending the letter does not mean the condition is cleared.

Build the File Before the Request Arrives

Many explanation letters can be avoided or made easier with a clean paper trail. Before and during the mortgage process:

  • Use traceable payments and transfers.
  • Keep every page of statements and closing records.
  • Tell the loan team before opening credit, moving funds, changing jobs, or accepting gift money.
  • Disclose other properties, obligations, and employment changes early.
  • Save documents for unusual deposits as soon as the transaction happens.

For related preparation, read the conditional approval versus clear to close guide, the employment changes before closing checklist, and the Florida mortgage gift funds guide.

The Bottom Line

A mortgage letter of explanation should answer one clear question with facts that match the documents. Short beats dramatic. Specific beats vague. Evidence beats confidence.

Ask the loan team what the underwriter needs, attach the right records, and confirm whether the response cleared the condition. A letter can help explain the file, but it cannot guarantee approval, clear to close, funding, or a closing date.
