Mortgage Wire Fraud at Florida Closings

By Dennis Ross, NMLS #2018381|October 30, 2026

Before you send money for an Orlando home closing, stop and verify where it is going. I would rather have a buyer make an extra call than trust a convincing email because everyone is trying to finish the deal.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau warns that mortgage closing scams target buyers near closing. A criminal pretends to be an agent or settlement professional and tries to redirect the buyer's funds. The message can look like part of a real conversation.[1]

A Familiar Email Is Not Enough

The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center describes business email compromise as a scam that can involve compromised legitimate email accounts and unauthorized fund transfers. It recommends verifying account-change requests with the intended recipient through a secondary channel.[4]

My recommendation: separate the message from the verification. Replying to the same thread to ask whether the instructions are real is not the independent check I want a buyer relying on.

Build Your Trusted Contact List Before Closing Week

The CFPB recommends writing down trusted contacts who can confirm the closing process and payment instructions.[1] Do that before the day you intend to move the money, not while someone is pressuring you to hit send.

I would ask the closing team these questions early:

  • Who is the intended recipient of the closing funds?
  • How should I obtain the instructions and verify them independently?
  • What trusted phone number should I use for that verification?
  • Who should confirm receipt after the transfer?

Keep the answers with your closing checklist. For the broader process, read what happens at a Florida mortgage closing and the final walkthrough and closing checklist.

Verify the Instructions Through a Separate Channel

The National Association of Realtors recommends obtaining wiring instructions from the recipient in person when possible. If you receive them another way, confirm them in person or by calling the recipient at a trusted number. It also advises calling a trusted source before and after wiring funds.[3]

For my buyers, the practical instruction is simple: use the contact you established independently, not a new number supplied in the message you are trying to check. Ask the closing professional to walk through the recipient and instructions with you before authorizing the transfer.

Treat Changed Instructions as a Reason to Stop

NAR flags last-minute changes by email or voicemail as suspicious.[3] The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center also recommends a secondary channel to verify requests to change account information with the intended recipient.[4]

Do not let a deadline become your verification method. If something changes, my recommendation is to stop the transfer and contact the closing team through your trusted channel. Ask them to resolve the discrepancy before proceeding. This is a safety check, not a promise that closing dates cannot change.

If You Already Sent Money, Call the Bank Immediately

The FBI says to contact the originating financial institution as soon as fraud is recognized and request a recall or reversal. It also directs victims to file a detailed complaint with the Internet Crime Complaint Center, including the requested banking information.[4]

Make that bank call immediately. Do not wait for an email reply or for the closing appointment. Tell your closing team through trusted contact information as well. Keep the messages and transfer confirmation available so you can supply the details requested by the bank and investigators.

A recall request is not a guarantee that the funds will be recovered. Fraud prevention and reporting do not replace advice from your bank, closing professional, or attorney about your situation.
