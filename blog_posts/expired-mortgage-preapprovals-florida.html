Expired Mortgage Preapprovals in Florida

By Dennis Ross, NMLS #2018381|November 14, 2026

Your preapproval letter expired while you were looking for a home. Before you ask someone to change the date, I would ask a better question: does the old review still describe your finances and the home you want to buy?

For Orlando buyers returning to the search, my recommendation is to refresh the file before the next offer, not after a seller accepts it. Bring the old letter, explain what changed, and ask the loan officer what needs another look.

Start With the Letter's Actual Expiration Date

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau describes a preapproval as tentative, based on assumptions, and not a guaranteed loan offer. Its consumer guide says letters can expire, typically after 30 to 60 days, and that lenders' processes differ.[1] That is general guidance, not a Florida rule setting one expiration date for every lender.

I would check the date printed on your own letter and ask the issuing lender whether it is still usable. Do not assume a letter from an earlier search is current because your target purchase price has not changed. Ask what review is needed for a replacement and how much time to allow.

Give the Loan Officer a Short Change List

My suggested starting point is a plain-language update, not a guess about whether you still qualify. I would tell the loan officer about any of these changes and ask which ones require documents or a new review:

  • A different employer, pay structure, work schedule, or self-employment situation.
  • New debt, a paid-off account, a different required payment, or a credit concern.
  • A change in available savings, the source of purchase funds, or a planned gift.
  • A different purchase price, property type, intended occupancy, or purchase timeline.

This is a conversation checklist, not a complete underwriting checklist. I would include changes even when they seem helpful. Let the loan officer decide what needs to be verified instead of leaving something out because you expect it to improve the file.

Refresh Documents Through the Lender's Secure Process

The CFPB recommends updating time-sensitive paperwork, including pay stubs and bank statements, and asking lenders what additional documents your situation needs. It also notes that self-employed or irregular-income borrowers may need more extensive income documentation.[2]

My recommendation is to ask for a current document request rather than resend an old packet and hope it covers everything. Ask which statement periods are needed, which income records to provide, and where to upload them securely. Do not post account numbers, Social Security numbers, tax returns, or unredacted statements into a public form or comment.

If you run a business, ask what current business records the reviewer wants before you start assembling them. For a related planning resource, read the Florida self-employed borrower documentation checklist. Your loan officer should confirm the requirements for your actual loan.

Ask About Credit Review Before Assuming Anything

The CFPB says lenders typically check credit before issuing a preapproval.[1] I would ask whether refreshing your file requires another credit review, what authorization is needed, and whether any fee applies. Do not assume renewing the letter means either an automatic new inquiry or no inquiry at all.

Bring up a planned car purchase, credit application, or debt payoff before acting. My recommendation is to have the loan officer review the proposed change with you, rather than make a financial move solely because you think it will help the mortgage. This article does not promise a credit-score result or a particular treatment by a lender.

Revisit the Budget, Not Just the Purchase Price

The CFPB cautions that your own spending comfort matters even if a lender preapproves a higher amount.[1] My recommendation is to return with two targets: the cash you are comfortable using and the ongoing housing cost you are comfortable carrying.

For a specific Orlando home, I would ask the loan officer for a current scenario using that property's available tax, insurance, and association information, with estimates and unknowns clearly labeled. Have the relevant professionals verify those inputs. Do not treat the housing cost discussed during your last search as a current quote.

I would also ask whether any rate was actually locked, what written terms apply, and what remains subject to change. Do not use the existence of a preapproval letter as proof of a current rate commitment. For the budget conversation, see why your preapproval amount is not your real homebuying budget.

Before Your Next Offer, Get Clear Answers

My suggested questions are simple: What did you review? What remains unverified? What changed in the proposed loan? When does the replacement letter expire? Does the property I am considering need a separate review? Ask what information can be shared with your real estate agent without exposing private financial documents.

My bottom line: do not ask for a newer date while relying on an older financial picture. Ask for an updated review, understand its limits, and use the resulting letter alongside a budget you can actually live with.

This is general mortgage education, not individualized financial, legal, tax, insurance, or underwriting advice. Guidelines and lender requirements can change. Consult your loan officer for current requirements. A preapproval is not a guarantee of eligibility, approval, funding, a rate, or a closing date.
