Florida Final Walkthrough and Closing Checklist

By Dennis Ross, NMLS #2018381|September 30, 2026

Your final walkthrough is not the place to discover that nobody checked the repair, the closing funds, or the plan for getting the keys.

For Orlando and Florida buyers, use two separate checklists: one for the house and one for the mortgage closing. A house that looks ready does not answer your financing questions. A scheduled signing does not answer your property questions.

The CFPB recommends checking that the home is acceptable and agreed repairs are complete before closing.[1] The detailed checks below are practical preparation suggestions, not a statement of your contract rights or a substitute for an inspection.

Before the Walkthrough: Bring the Actual Agreement

Ask your real estate agent to have the purchase agreement, repair addenda, included-item list, and relevant inspection photos available. Compare the house with the actual written agreement, not what someone remembers discussing.

  • Ask when the walkthrough will happen and whether utilities will be on for testing.
  • List every agreed repair and the evidence you want to review.
  • Confirm which appliances, fixtures, remotes, and other items are supposed to stay.
  • Clarify the agreed possession arrangement, especially if the seller will remain after closing.
  • Save the agent's, settlement agent's, and loan officer's numbers before the final day.

Do not assume every Florida contract gives buyers the same remedy, deadline, or possession terms. Questions about enforcement, withholding funds, cancellation, or liability belong with a qualified Florida real estate attorney.

Final Walkthrough Checklist for the House

Walk room by room. Take dated photos of concerns and compare them with earlier photos. These are suggested observations to discuss with your agent; they are not a professional inspection or a guarantee that hidden defects will be found.

  • Agreed repairs: Look at each repair location. Ask for receipts or completion records when relevant. A receipt is not proof that the work is adequate.
  • Water: If safe and permitted, run faucets, check under sinks, and flush toilets. Look for new stains, damp flooring, or visible leaks.
  • Air conditioning: Ask to verify basic operation. If performance is questionable, request professional evaluation rather than guessing from a thermostat reading.
  • Included appliances: Confirm the expected appliances remain. Check basic operation where practical and safe.
  • Doors and windows: Look for obvious damage and check accessible locks, latches, and garage controls.
  • Move-out condition: Note new wall damage, missing fixtures, leftover belongings, or debris. Compare concerns with the agreed terms.
  • Exterior: Look for visible new damage, standing water, or changes around the property since your earlier visit.
  • Access items: Make a list of keys, mailbox keys, remotes, gate credentials, and any access instructions to confirm at handoff.

For a Florida condo, add a separate access list for parking, storage, building entry, and association onboarding. Ask the association or management contact what you need. Do not assume those details are handled just because the lender reviewed the project.

What If You Find a Problem?

Photograph the issue and contact your agent before signing. If the concern involves property condition, an agreed repair, a seller credit, or a proposed change to the transaction, bring the loan officer and settlement agent into the conversation as well.

Do not make a private cash arrangement with the seller or assume a repair credit can be added without review. Ask the loan team whether the proposed solution affects loan documents, lender requirements, or timing. Ask an attorney about your contractual choices.

A useful message is specific: identify the location, describe what you observed, attach photos, and ask who must review it before you proceed. Avoid diagnosing a structural or safety issue yourself.

For related preparation, read conditional approval versus clear to close. Neither a walkthrough nor a signing appointment should be treated as a promise of funding.

Mortgage Closing Checklist: Review the Numbers First

For most standard home-purchase mortgages, you generally must receive the Closing Disclosure at least three business days before closing. It sets out the final terms, projected payments, and closing costs. Confirm the applicable timing with your lender instead of counting calendar days yourself.[2]

Use the CFPB's Closing Disclosure explainer to compare the loan amount, loan product, rate, payment, costs, and cash to close with your latest Loan Estimate. Ask why anything differs.[2]

My practical preparation list is simple:

  • Ask the lender to identify anything still required from you.
  • Confirm the final amount due and the settlement agent's accepted payment method.
  • Ask the loan team to confirm the insurance documents and effective date needed for the file.
  • Ask which charges will be collected through escrow and which you must pay separately.
  • Tell the loan officer about any employment, debt, asset, or contract change before acting.

If closing funds involve a recent transfer or unusual deposit, review how large bank deposits affect mortgage approval and ask what documentation your specific file requires.

Verify Wire Instructions Outside the Email

The CFPB warns that criminals may impersonate transaction participants and send false closing-payment instructions. Before wiring money, call a trusted contact using a phone number you independently established earlier. Do not use the number in a suspicious message. Treat a last-minute change as a reason to stop and verify, not hurry.[3]

If you think you sent money to the wrong account, contact your bank immediately and alert the legitimate settlement agent. Do not wait for a reply from the email sender. The CFPB also recommends reporting suspected fraud to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center.[3]

At Signing: Do Not Let the Schedule Replace Review

The CFPB recommends bringing identification, your Closing Disclosure, and the required closing-payment evidence, and reviewing documents for accuracy.[4] Confirm the exact identification and signing requirements with your settlement agent ahead of time.

Ask for explanations of unfamiliar terms. If the paperwork does not reflect what you expected, identify the difference before signing. A moving truck reservation is not a reason to ignore an unanswered question.

Ask who will confirm that signing, lender funding, settlement requirements, and the agreed key handoff are complete. Do not assume the act of signing means you can immediately move in. Your transaction participants should explain the sequence for your purchase.

After Closing: Keep the Records and Confirm the First Payment

Keep the complete closing packet and confirm the first payment date, payment destination, and insurance contacts. Include separately paid association dues, taxes, or insurance in your household plan where applicable.[4]

My recommendation: keep a separate home folder for the walkthrough photos, repair records, appliance information, and access details. Store banking and identity documents securely rather than forwarding the entire closing packet casually.

The Bottom Line

Check the house against the written agreement. Check the loan against the actual documents. Verify the money transfer independently. Get unresolved questions in front of the right person before you sign.
