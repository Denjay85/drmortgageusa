FHA Condo Single-Unit Approval Basics for Florida Homebuyers

By Dennis Ross, NMLS #2018381 | August 31, 2026

You found a Florida condo that fits your budget, but the project does not appear on FHA's approved condominium list. That does not always end the FHA conversation.

FHA's single-unit approval process can allow an eligible unit in a condominium project that is not FHA approved to be reviewed at the loan level. It is a narrow path, not a shortcut around condo underwriting.

The borrower, unit, project, association, insurance, finances, and documents still have to satisfy FHA requirements and the lender's review. Buyers should start that work before paying for an appraisal or giving up contract protections.

What FHA Single-Unit Approval Means

HUD Handbook 4000.1 defines single-unit approval as approval of a unit in a condominium project that is not an approved condominium project. The lender confirms the project is not on FHA's approved list at case number assignment, then reviews the unit and project under FHA's loan-level requirements.

This is different from full project approval. A successful loan-level review does not turn the entire association into an FHA-approved condominium project. It addresses the specific unit and mortgage being submitted.

Start With the FHA Condo Search

Before discussing single-unit approval, check the official HUD Condominium Projects search. The search allows users to look for FHA-approved projects by location, name, or status.

Do not rely only on a listing description, an old approval letter, or an association manager's memory. The lender must confirm the project's status at the required point in the FHA process.

If the project is approved, the lender follows FHA's requirements for units in approved projects. If it is not approved, the lender can determine whether the single-unit path is available. Site condominiums are a separate category and may not require project approval or single-unit approval when they meet FHA's definition and standards.

Basic Property Gates

Under the current HUD handbook section reviewed for this article, the lender must confirm several basic facts before the unit can use the single-unit approval path:

  • The condominium project is not on FHA's approved project list at case number assignment.
  • The complete project or legal phase has certificates of occupancy or equivalent documentation for all units.
  • The subject unit has a certificate of occupancy issued at least one year earlier or has been occupied.
  • The project has at least five units.
  • The unit is not a manufactured home.
  • The project does not contain FHA ineligible characteristics.
  • The project meets applicable legal restrictions on conveyance.

These are threshold items, not the whole review. Clearing them does not guarantee approval.

The Borrower Still Has a Separate FHA Review

Single-unit approval solves no borrower qualification problem. HUD's handbook says the mortgage application must receive an Accept from the TOTAL Mortgage Scorecard. If the loan is manually underwritten, the handbook section limits the maximum loan-to-value ratio to 90 percent.

The lender still reviews credit, income, employment, assets, debt, occupancy, property value, and every other requirement that applies to the FHA mortgage. Lender overlays may be more restrictive than FHA's baseline.

Project Numbers Matter

Owner occupancy

For single-unit approval, HUD's handbook requires the project to have an owner occupancy percentage of at least 50 percent. The calculation follows FHA definitions, so a casual estimate from the association may not be enough.

Existing FHA loan concentration

FHA also limits how many FHA-insured mortgages can be in a project through the single-unit path. The handbook states that projects with 20 or more units may be restricted when FHA concentration exceeds 10 percent. For projects with fewer than 20 units, the number of FHA-insured mortgages cannot exceed two.

Association delinquencies and reserves

The lender reviews the association's financial stability. The handbook section includes reserve funding, separate operating and reserve accounts, and a limit on units more than 60 days past due for association dues or special assessments. A reserve study may affect how the reserve requirement is evaluated.

Insurance Is a Real Florida Closing Risk

The condo project must meet FHA insurance standards and applicable state and local requirements. Depending on the project and master policy, the review can involve hazard, liability, fidelity, flood, and unit-level walls-in coverage.

A certificate showing that some policy exists does not answer every underwriting question. The lender may need the full policy, replacement-cost evidence, deductibles, named-insured details, coverage for common elements, flood information, and an HO-6 policy for the buyer.

Florida insurance issues can stall a clean borrower file. Read how Florida homeowners insurance can affect mortgage approval before treating insurance as a last-week item.

Documents the Lender May Need

FHA uses form HUD-9991, FHA Condominium Loan Level/Single-Unit Approval Questionnaire, to collect loan, project, association, and unit information. The lender may also need supporting records such as:

  • Recorded declaration, bylaws, covenants, conditions, restrictions, amendments, and project plans.
  • Evidence that control of the association transferred from the developer when required.
  • The current budget, financial statements, reserve information, and unit delinquency data.
  • Master insurance policies, certificates, replacement-cost evidence, and flood documents when applicable.
  • Association meeting minutes and information about litigation, repairs, special assessments, or financial distress.
  • Occupancy, unit ownership, commercial-space, and existing FHA loan data.
  • Certificates of occupancy or equivalent records.

The exact package depends on the project facts and current FHA guidance. Missing or conflicting association records can stop the review even when the unit itself looks fine.

Common Reasons the Path Gets Hard

  • The project has fewer than five units.
  • Owner occupancy is below FHA's required level.
  • The project already reached the applicable FHA concentration limit.
  • The association has too many delinquent units or weak reserve support.
  • Master insurance does not satisfy the required review.
  • Litigation involves safety, structural soundness, habitability, or functional use.
  • Repairs, structural concerns, or special assessments create unresolved project risk.
  • Project records are incomplete, outdated, unsigned, or inconsistent.
  • The borrower receives a manual underwriting result and the proposed loan exceeds the applicable loan-to-value limit.

For a broader project-risk review, use the Florida condo financing red flags guide. If the association has a pending charge, read the Florida condo special assessment guide.

A Better Offer Strategy for Orlando Buyers

  1. Send the exact project name, address, phase, and unit number to the loan officer before writing the offer when possible.
  2. Check the official HUD project search, but let the lender confirm the status and correct review path.
  3. Ask the association or management company whether it will complete HUD-9991 and provide supporting documents.
  4. Request current insurance, budget, reserves, delinquency, litigation, inspection, repair, and special-assessment information.
  5. Keep financing and condo-document protections that fit the actual review risk.
  6. Build enough time into the contract for the lender and association to complete the review.
  7. Do not order expensive extras based only on a verbal statement that the project should qualify.

Questions to Ask Before You Spend Money

  • Is the project currently FHA approved, unapproved, expired, rejected, or in another status?
  • Does this unit appear eligible for single-unit approval?
  • Has the lender handled an FHA single-unit approval in this project before?
  • Who will complete HUD-9991, and how quickly?
  • What association documents are still missing?
  • Do owner occupancy and FHA concentration fit the current rules?
  • Are insurance, reserves, delinquencies, litigation, repairs, or assessments raising concerns?
  • Are lender overlays stricter than FHA's published baseline?
  • What contract deadlines could arrive before the condo review is complete?

The Bottom Line

An unapproved Florida condo project is not automatically an FHA dead end. Single-unit approval may provide a loan-level path, but only when the borrower, unit, project, association, finances, insurance, and documentation satisfy the applicable review.

The clean move is early verification. Check the project, collect the association package, and let the lender identify the real blockers before your deposit, appraisal fee, and contract deadlines are exposed.

This article is general mortgage education, not legal, tax, insurance, financial, or association advice. FHA guidance, lender overlays, forms, and project facts can change. Approval and eligibility require review of the complete borrower file, unit, project, and current documentation.
