Florida Home Equity Loan vs Cash-Out Refinance

By Dennis Ross, NMLS #2018381|October 3, 2026

Before you borrow against your Florida home, ask one question: do you need to replace your current mortgage, or do you only need additional cash?

Those are different decisions. For an Orlando homeowner, my recommendation is to compare a home equity loan and a cash-out refinance using the same cash goal, not just the rate printed at the top of each quote.

Home Equity Loan vs Cash-Out Refinance: The Difference

A home equity loan typically provides a lump sum that you repay through regular payments. When you already have a mortgage, a second mortgage adds another loan secured by your home rather than replacing the first one.[1][2]

A cash-out refinance replaces your existing mortgage with a larger mortgage and releases cash from the transaction. Closing costs often apply. The CFPB recommends paying particular attention when the replacement rate is higher than your current mortgage rate.[2]

My starting point: identify the loan you already have before deciding what to add or replace. Bring your current statement, remaining term, and a clear explanation of what the money is for.

Do Not Confuse a Home Equity Loan With a HELOC

The FTC explains that home equity loans usually provide money upfront and typically have a fixed APR and repayment term. A HELOC is a revolving credit line secured by the home, typically with a variable APR. Confirm the actual product and payment structure in the offer.[3]

For that separate comparison, read HELOC vs home equity loan in Florida. This article focuses on a lump-sum home equity loan versus replacing the first mortgage through a cash-out refinance.

When Preserving the First Mortgage Deserves a Look

The CFPB's archived December 2022 guidance identifies home equity borrowing as an alternative to replacing a lower-rate first mortgage with a higher-rate cash-out refinance. That is a structural comparison, not a claim about today's rates.[4]

My recommendation is to request a second-loan comparison when you want to preserve the existing mortgage. Do not stop at that preference. Ask the loan officer to show the combined obligations and the payoff schedule for both loans.

  • What stays unchanged on the existing mortgage?
  • What is the new loan's full payment schedule?
  • Are any charges paid upfront, deducted from proceeds, or financed?
  • What would you still owe on each loan at your expected sale date?
  • What happens if your household income falls or a major repair arrives?

Keeping your first loan is a comparison point, not proof that the second loan is affordable or less expensive.

When to Put a Cash-Out Refinance Beside It

I would also request a refinance comparison if changing the existing mortgage is already part of your goal. Ask the loan officer to identify exactly what is being replaced, what is being paid off, and what remains after closing.

In a cash-out refinance, the new mortgage has its own rate and terms. The CFPB cautions that a replacement mortgage can be costly when it replaces a lower-rate loan.[2][4] Do not assume a refinance is an improvement simply because cash is available.

For the closing review, use the Florida cash-out refinance closing cost checklist. Ask for a current, itemized explanation for your actual file rather than carrying over an old quote.

Compare the Same Cash Goal and the Same Time Horizon

Here is the comparison I recommend requesting. It is a shopping checklist, not a quoted loan scenario or a promise of savings.

  1. Net usable cash: Ask how much reaches you after payoffs and transaction costs. Use that same goal for both proposals.
  2. Total monthly obligation: Compare the existing mortgage plus the proposed home equity loan against the refinance proposal. Identify taxes, insurance, association charges, and any items shown separately.
  3. Upfront and financed costs: Request a line-by-line explanation of each charge and how it is paid.
  4. Repayment schedule: Ask when each loan ends and whether the payment structure includes interest-only periods or a balloon.
  5. Expected holding period: Request remaining balances and projected financing costs at the date you realistically expect to sell or pay off the loans.

Do not choose from a monthly-payment screenshot alone. My recommendation is to review the payment, costs, and projected remaining debt together, with the assumptions written down.

Debt Consolidation Does Not Remove the Debt

The CFPB warns that borrowing against home equity to repay other balances replaces those debts with a new loan. Using the home as collateral can put it at risk if you cannot repay. Extending repayment can also mean paying interest for longer.[1]

Before using either option for consolidation, my suggestion is to write down which balances would be paid off and how you would avoid rebuilding them. Ask a qualified financial counselor for help if the underlying budget problem remains. A new mortgage should not be treated as a solution to ongoing overspending.

Equity Is Not an Approval

The FTC says borrowing terms and amounts depend on factors including income, credit history, and the home's market value. It also warns that failure to repay a home-secured loan can lead to foreclosure.[3]

For your Florida property, ask the loan officer which documentation and property review the specific lender requires. Have current mortgage statements and information about any other liens available. Do not assume an online home-value estimate establishes how much you can borrow.

I also recommend keeping your local insurance, taxes, association obligations, and planned repairs in the household budget review. Ask the relevant professionals to verify those amounts. Do not assume borrowing capacity means the payment fits your life.

The Bottom Line for Florida Homeowners

Start with what you need the money to accomplish. Compare preserving the current mortgage with replacing it. Request the same net cash goal, a complete cost explanation, and a realistic payoff comparison. Be willing to decide not to borrow.
